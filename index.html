What you need to know already

Nothing. Really.

Every math tool used here (multiplying probabilities, Σ and Π, logarithms, derivatives, solving an equation) is explained in Part 2 with small examples. If you know them, skim. If not, go slowly.

How to study this page: read one part, then close your eyes and say the key idea out loud. Every part ends with a “remember it” line for that.

Part 1

The question. A new Mirana joins your team.

You don't know how good she is. You watch her shoot 10 Sacred Arrows. H means hit, M means miss:

HHMHHHMHMH

7 hits, 3 misses. What is her real hit rate? Your gut says 70%. Your gut is right, and this lecture shows why.

Why learn a method if the gut already knows? Because for other questions the gut has no answer. “The enemy ganked you after 2, 5, 3 and 6 minutes. How often do they gank?” For that you need a method that always works. That method is maximum likelihood.

DataWhat you actually saw. Here: the 10 arrows.
ModelA rule for how the data is produced. Here: “each arrow hits with the same chance, independently.”
Parameter θThe unknown number inside the model, like a knob. Here: θ = her true hit rate. θ is read “theta”.
Estimate θ̂Our best guess for θ from the data. The hat ^ means “estimated”. Read “theta hat”.

Remember it: the model has a knob θ. We see data. We want to turn the knob to the position that fits the data best.

Part 2

Math toolbox. Five tools, each with a tiny example.

Tool 1 · Multiply for “and”

Independent events: multiply their probabilities

P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B)

“Independent” means one arrow does not change the next one. Then the chance of a whole sequence is the product of the single chances.

Example. Hit rate θ. Hit, then miss: θ · (1 − θ). If θ = 0.7: 0.7 · 0.3 = 0.21.

Try it: chance of H, H, M if θ = 0.5?

0.5 · 0.5 · 0.5 = 0.125.

Tool 2 · Powers, Σ and Π

Short ways to write long sums and products

θ · θ · θ = θ³    Σ xᵢ = x₁ + x₂ + …    Π aᵢ = a₁ · a₂ · …

Σ (big sigma) means “add them all”. Π (big pi) means “multiply them all”. The little i just counts 1, 2, 3, …, n.

Example. Data 2, 5, 3: Σ xᵢ = 10, Π xᵢ = 30. Seven hits and three misses: θ7(1 − θ)3.

Try it: for 4, 7, 5, 8, what is Σ xᵢ and the average x̄?

Σ xᵢ = 24, x̄ = 24/4 = 6. The average is “Σ divided by n”.

Tool 3 · Logarithm

log turns products into sums

log(a·b) = log a + log b    log(aᵏ) = k·log a    log(eᵃ) = a

In this course “log” is the natural log (ln). Two facts matter: the three rules above, and log always goes up: if a < b then log a < log b. Logs of numbers between 0 and 1 are negative. That is normal.

Example. log(0.7 · 0.3) = log 0.7 + log 0.3 = −0.357 − 1.204 = −1.561, and indeed log 0.21 = −1.561.

Try it: write log(θ⁷(1 − θ)³) as a sum.

7 log θ + 3 log(1 − θ). Product → sum, power → factor in front.

Tool 4 · Derivative

The derivative is the slope. At the top of a hill it is 0.

constant c→ 0
c · θ→ c
log θ→ 1/θ
log(1 − θ)→ −1/(1 − θ) (outside 1/(1−θ) times inside (1−θ)′ = −1)
1/v→ −1/v²
a sum→ derivative of each part, added
Try it: derivative of 7 log θ + 3 log(1 − θ)?

7/θ − 3/(1 − θ).

Tool 5 · Solving a fraction equation

Get rid of fractions by multiplying both sides

start7/θ = 3/(1 − θ)the kind of equation MLE gives you
× θ(1−θ)7(1 − θ) = 3θmultiply both sides by both denominators; they cancel
expand7 − 7θ = 3θmultiply out the bracket
+ 7θ7 = 10θcollect all θ on one side
÷ 10θ = 0.7done

Remember it: multiply for “and”, log turns × into +, the top of a hill has slope 0.

Part 3

The likelihood. Try every knob position and ask how well it explains the data.

Here is the whole idea. Pretend for a moment that you know θ. Then Tool 1 tells you the probability of seeing exactly our 10 arrows (7 H, 3 M):

L(θ) = θ7 (1 − θ)3

Now do this for many values of θ. The table shows the result. The value of θ that makes our data most probable is θ = 0.7. That is the maximum likelihood estimate.

Important: the data stays fixed (our 10 arrows). Only θ moves. L(θ) is “how well this guess explains the data”, a score for each guess. It is not the probability that θ is true; the scores do not even add up to 1.

guess θL(θ) = θ⁷(1−θ)³
0.30.000075
0.50.000977
0.60.001792
0.70.002224
0.80.001678
0.90.000478

Check one yourself: for θ = 0.5, 0.57 · 0.53 = 0.510 ≈ 0.000977.

Remember it: likelihood = how well a guess explains the data. MLE = the guess with the best score.

Part 4

Why we take the log. Two reasons, one guarantee.

Reason 1

The numbers get tiny

10 arrows already give 0.0022. With 1000 arrows L is so close to 0 that a computer just says 0. Logs keep the numbers normal: log 0.0022 ≈ −6.1.

Reason 2

Sums are easier than products

A derivative of a long product is messy. A derivative of a sum is easy: one part at a time (Tool 4).

The guarantee

The top stays in the same place

Log always goes up (Tool 3). So whichever θ gives the biggest L also gives the biggest log L. We call it ℓ(θ) = log L(θ).

Check it with numbers: ℓ(θ) = 7 log θ + 3 log(1 − θ)

ℓ(0.5) = −6.93ℓ(0.7) = −6.11 ← highestℓ(0.9) = −7.65

Negative numbers: the highest one is the one closest to zero. Same winner as in Part 3.

Remember it: take the log because it turns × into + and does not move the top.

Part 5

The recipe. The same four steps, every single time.

01
Likelihood

Multiply the probabilities of all data points

L(θ) = p(x₁|θ) · p(x₂|θ) · … · p(xₙ|θ). Tool 1.

02
Log

Take the log: products become sums

ℓ(θ) = Σ log p(xᵢ|θ). Tool 3. Drop terms without θ: their derivative is 0 anyway.

03
Derivative

Set the derivative to zero and solve

ℓ′(θ) = 0. Tools 4 and 5. The solution is θ̂.

04
Check

Make sure it is a top, not a valley

ℓ″(θ̂) < 0 means the slope is falling: a top. Also look for tops at the edges.

Remember it (Dota version): Last hit, Last hit, Deny, Check the minimap → Likelihood, Log, Derivative, Check.

Part 6

Mirana, fully worked. Every line, with the reason next to it.

The model. Each arrow hits with probability θ and misses with probability 1 − θ, independently. We saw k hits in n arrows (k = 7, n = 10). We do it with letters, so the answer works for any numbers.

1 · LL(θ) = θk (1 − θ)n−kTool 1: multiply θ once for every hit and (1 − θ) once for every miss; Tool 2 writes it as powers
2 · logℓ(θ) = k log θ + (n − k) log(1 − θ)Tool 3: log of a product = sum of logs; the power comes down in front
3 · Dℓ′(θ) = k/θ − (n − k)/(1 − θ)Tool 4: log θ → 1/θ and log(1 − θ) → −1/(1 − θ)
set 0k/θ = (n − k)/(1 − θ)at the top the slope is zero; move the minus part to the other side
× θ(1−θ)k(1 − θ) = (n − k)θTool 5: clear both fractions
expandk − kθ = nθ − kθmultiply out both brackets
+ kθk = nθthe −kθ on both sides cancels
÷ nθ̂ = k / n = 7/10 = 0.7the MLE
4 · Cℓ″(θ) = −k/θ² − (n − k)/(1 − θ)² < 0both parts are negative, so it is a top ✓
Why it makes sense: a tug of war

Hits pull θ up. Misses pull θ down.

Look at the derivative again: k/θ comes from the hits and is positive, so it pushes θ higher. (n − k)/(1 − θ) comes from the misses and pushes θ lower. The answer is where both pulls are equally strong. That balance point is exactly the share of hits.

If θ were 0.9, three misses would be very surprising, so the misses pull hard. If θ were 0.5, seven hits would be a bit lucky, so the hits pull. They balance at 0.7.

k = 7n = 10θ̂ = 0.70

Remember it: Bernoulli MLE = hits ÷ tries, because that is where the pull of the hits and the pull of the misses balance.

Part 7

Time between ganks. A new model, the same four steps.

What is this model? Some things happen at random moments: ganks, deaths, Roshan attempts. The exponential distribution describes the waiting time x between them:

p(x | λ) = λ e−λx, x ≥ 0

λ (“lambda”) is the rate: how many events per minute on average. Big λ means short waits. The average wait is 1/λ: if they gank 0.25 times per minute, you wait 4 minutes on average.

Data: the enemy ganked you after 2, 5, 3 and 6 minutes. So n = 4 and Σ xᵢ = 16.

Short waits are most likely; long waits are rare. The dots are our four waits.

1 · LL(λ) = λe−λx₁ · λe−λx₂ · … = λn e−λ Σxᵢn copies of λ give λⁿ; and eᵃ · eᵇ = eᵃ⁺ᵇ, so the exponents add up
2 · logℓ(λ) = n log λ − λ ΣxᵢTool 3: log λⁿ = n log λ and log eᵃ = a
3 · Dℓ′(λ) = n/λ − Σxᵢlog λ → 1/λ; λ · Σxᵢ → Σxᵢ (Σxᵢ is just a number)
set 0n/λ = Σxᵢslope zero at the top
solveλ̂ = n / Σxᵢ = 4/16 = 0.25multiply by λ, divide by Σxᵢ
4 · Cℓ″(λ) = −n/λ² < 0a top ✓ (this is Week 4, Question 1)

Remember it: λ̂ = n/Σxᵢ = 1/x̄. One over the average wait. Average wait 4 minutes → rate 1/4 per minute.

Part 8

Losses before a win. The model that was on both finals.

What is this model? Every game you win with probability q. The number of losses k before your first win follows the geometric distribution:

p(k | q) = (1 − q)k q, k = 0, 1, 2, …

Read it like a story: you lose k times, each with probability (1 − q), and then win once, with probability q. Multiply (Tool 1).

Data: on four days the losses before the first win were 2, 0, 3, 1. So n = 4 days and S = Σkᵢ = 6 losses.

With q = 0.4, zero losses is most likely, and each extra loss is less likely.

1 · LL(q) = Π (1 − q)kᵢ q = qn (1 − q)Sone q per day (n of them); the (1 − q) powers add up to S = Σkᵢ
2 · logℓ(q) = n log q + S log(1 − q)Tool 3. It looks exactly like Mirana with k → n and n − k → S
3 · Dℓ′(q) = n/q − S/(1 − q)Tool 4
set 0n(1 − q) = Sq → n = (n + S)qTool 5: clear fractions, expand, collect q
solveq̂ = n / (n + S) = 4/10 = 0.4wins ÷ all games played
The 2026 version. If you count games including the win, y = k + 1, the model is λ(1 − λ)y−1 and the MLE is n/Σyᵢ. Same answer: Σyᵢ = S + n = 10 games, 4 of them wins.

Remember it: geometric MLE = wins ÷ all games. Again a share, like Mirana.

Part 9

Last hits on a bell curve. Two parameters at once.

What is this model? The Gaussian (normal, bell curve) describes numbers that cluster around a center. It has two knobs: μ (“mu”) is the center, σ² (“sigma squared”) is the spread, the average squared distance from the center.

p(x | μ, σ²) = (1/√(2πσ²)) · exp(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²))

Don't be scared by the formula. The only part that matters for μ is (x − μ)²: the further x is from the center, the smaller the probability.

Data: last hits at 10 minutes in three games: 50, 60, 70.

Step A · the log-likelihood

log of onelog p(xᵢ) = −½ log(2π) − ½ log σ² − (xᵢ − μ)²/(2σ²)Tool 3: log(1/√a) = −½ log a and log(exp(b)) = b
sum allℓ(μ, σ²) = const − (n/2) log σ² − (1/(2σ²)) Σ(xᵢ − μ)²add n copies; −½ log(2π) has no parameter, call it “const”

Step B · find μ̂ (treat σ² as a fixed number)

3 · D∂ℓ/∂μ = (1/σ²) Σ(xᵢ − μ)derivative of −(x − μ)²/(2σ²) in μ: bring the 2 down, inside derivative is −1, so the minus signs cancel
set 0Σ(xᵢ − μ) = 0 → Σxᵢ − nμ = 0multiply by σ²; Σμ = nμ because μ appears n times
solveμ̂ = Σxᵢ / n = x̄ = (50 + 60 + 70)/3 = 60the average

Step C · find σ̂² (write v = σ² to keep it simple)

rewriteℓ(v) = const − (n/2) log v − S/(2v), S = Σ(xᵢ − μ̂)²S is just a number: 100 + 0 + 100 = 200
3 · Dℓ′(v) = −n/(2v) + S/(2v²)log v → 1/v; 1/v → −1/v², and minus times minus is plus
set 0−nv + S = 0multiply everything by 2v²
solveσ̂² = S/n = (1/n) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = 200/3 ≈ 66.7the average squared distance to the center
On the exam (2025 Final 4(e)): in linear regression the same Step C gives the noise variance σ̂² = (1/n) Σ (yᵢ − ŵᵀxᵢ)². Divide by n, not n − 1.

Remember it: best center = average; best spread = average squared distance from that average.

Part 10

When the derivative fails. Look at the picture instead.

What is this model? An imaginary hero deals random damage, any value between 0 and θ equally likely. That is the uniform distribution: p(x | θ) = 1/θ for 0 ≤ x ≤ θ, and 0 outside.

Data: hits of 120, 85 and 143. What is the max damage θ?

  1. Likelihood. If θ is at least 143, every hit is possible: L(θ) = (1/θ)³ = θ−3.
  2. If θ is below 143, the hit of 143 is impossible, so L(θ) = 0.
  3. Derivative? −3θ−4 is never 0. The usual step 3 gives nothing.
  4. Picture. L is 0 up to 143, jumps up, then only falls. So the top is right at the jump.

θ̂ = max xᵢ = 143

Why can't θ be bigger? A bigger θ spreads the probability over more values, so each hit we saw becomes less likely.

Remember it: if the slope is never zero, draw L. The top can be at an edge. (Week 4, “German tanks”.)

Explain it back: say each answer out loud before opening it

What is a likelihood, in one sentence?

For a fixed set of data, it gives every possible parameter value a score: how probable the data would be if that value were true.

Why is it allowed to maximize log L instead of L?

Log always goes up, so the same θ is at the top of both curves. And log turns the product into a sum, which is easy to differentiate.

Why is the derivative zero at the MLE?

The MLE is the top of the ℓ hill. At the top the curve is flat: going left it rises, going right it falls, and in between the slope is exactly 0.

Why is the Bernoulli MLE exactly k/n?

The derivative is k/θ − (n − k)/(1 − θ): hits pull θ up, misses pull it down. They balance exactly when θ = k/n.

What is the weakness of MLE?

With little data it believes the data too much: 3 hits out of 3 gives θ̂ = 1, “never misses”. The next lecture adds a prior to fix this.

Check yourself

Five questions. Paper first, then open.

1. Mirana hits 3 of 3 arrows. What is the MLE? Do you believe it?

θ̂ = 3/3 = 1: “she never misses”. With so little data that is clearly too confident. This is the overfitting problem of MLE, and the reason the Beta prior (Day 3) and MAP exist.

2. Minutes between ganks: 4, 4, 7. What is λ̂?

λ̂ = n / Σ xᵢ = 3/15 = 0.2 ganks per minute, one every 5 minutes.

3. Losses before your first win on three days: 1, 1, 4. What is q̂?

q̂ = n / (n + S) = 3 / (3 + 6) = 1/3. 3 wins in 9 games.

4. Last hits: 40, 50, 60, 70. What are μ̂ and σ̂²?

μ̂ = 55. σ̂² = (225 + 25 + 25 + 225)/4 = 125. Divide by n = 4.

5. Why may we maximize log L instead of L?

Log is always increasing, so the θ that maximizes L also maximizes log L. Sums are easier to differentiate than products, and tiny products like 0.0000001 don't underflow on a computer.

Practice · 30–40 min

Practice problems. Recipe in hand, pen on paper.

Seven Dota tasks. Write all four steps for each one, even when you can guess the answer. On the exam the steps give the points.

01Easy

Pudge hooks

Pudge throws 18 hooks and lands 6. Model each hook as Bernoulli(θ). Write L(θ), ℓ(θ), the derivative, and find θ̂.

Hint

Exactly Part 6 with k = 6 and n = 18.

Solution
  • L(θ) = θ6(1 − θ)12, ℓ(θ) = 6 log θ + 12 log(1 − θ)
  • 6/θ − 12/(1 − θ) = 0 → 6(1 − θ) = 12θ
  • θ̂ = 6/18 = 1/3
02Easy

Minutes between your deaths

In one game, the minutes between your deaths were 3, 7, 2, 8. Model them as Exponential(λ). Find λ̂ and say in words what it means.

Hint

λ̂ = n / Σ xᵢ (Part 7).

Solution
  • Σ xᵢ = 20, n = 4, so λ̂ = 4/20 = 0.2 deaths per minute
  • In words: on average you die once every 1/λ̂ = 5 minutes.
03Medium

A new model: kills per game

Your kills in four games: 4, 7, 5, 8. Use the Poisson model p(x | λ) = λx e−λ / x!. Find λ̂ with the recipe.

Hint

log p(x | λ) = x log λ − λ − log x!. The last term has no λ, so drop it.

Solution
  • ℓ(λ) = (Σ xᵢ) log λ − nλ + const
  • ℓ′(λ) = Σ xᵢ / λ − n = 0
  • λ̂ = x̄ = 24/4 = 6 kills per game. ℓ″ = −Σ xᵢ/λ² < 0 ✓
04Medium

Games until the first win (2026 version)

On four days you needed 3, 1, 2, 4 games to get your first win, including the win. Model: p(y | λ) = λ(1 − λ)y−1, y = 1, 2, …. Derive λ̂.

Hint

L(λ) = λn(1 − λ)Σ(yᵢ − 1). Then it looks like Part 8.

Solution
  • ℓ(λ) = n log λ + (Σ yᵢ − n) log(1 − λ)
  • n/λ − (Σ yᵢ − n)/(1 − λ) = 0
  • λ̂ = n / Σ yᵢ = 4/10 = 0.4: 4 wins in 10 games.
05Medium

GPM over five games

Your GPM: 400, 450, 500, 550, 600. Assume a Gaussian. Find μ̂ and σ̂². Why do we divide by 5 and not by 4?

Hint

Part 9: μ̂ = x̄, σ̂² = (1/n) Σ (xᵢ − x̄)².

Solution
  • μ̂ = 500
  • σ̂² = (10000 + 2500 + 0 + 2500 + 10000)/5 = 5000, σ̂ ≈ 70.7
  • The derivative of the log-likelihood gives exactly 1/n. Dividing by n − 1 is a different (unbiased) estimator, not the MLE.
06Exam-style · like 2025 Final 5

Naive Bayes by counting

Six games, written as (result, got first blood, won mid lane): (W,1,1), (W,1,0), (L,0,0), (W,0,1), (L,1,0), (L,0,0). (a) Find all MLEs: π̂W, θ̂ for each feature and class. (b) Classify a new game (1, 1). (c) What problem do you see?

Hint

Each MLE is a count ÷ a total: π̂W = #W / 6, θ̂FB|W = #(W with first blood) / #W.

Solution
  • (a) π̂W = π̂L = 3/6 = 0.5. Win: θ̂FB = 2/3, θ̂mid = 2/3. Loss: θ̂FB = 1/3, θ̂mid = 0/3 = 0.
  • (b) W: 0.5 · 2/3 · 2/3 ≈ 0.22. L: 0.5 · 1/3 · 0 = 0. Predict W.
  • (c) One zero count makes class L impossible for every game with mid won. That is MLE overfitting. Priors fix it (Days 3–6).
07Challenge · like 2025 Final 6

A Markov chain from two games

Your mid's states each 5 minutes: F = farm, T = fight, P = push. Game 1: F F T P. Game 2: F T T F. (a) MLE of the start distribution. (b) MLE of the transition probabilities out of F and out of T. (c) P(X₂ = P | X₀ = F).

Hint

Count only transitions inside each game. Row F = counts of F→F, F→T, F→P divided by all transitions that leave F. For (c), go through every middle state.

Solution
  • (a) Both games start in F: π̂(F) = 1, π̂(T) = π̂(P) = 0
  • (b) From F: F→F 1, F→T 2, F→P 0 → (1/3, 2/3, 0). From T: T→T 1, T→P 1, T→F 1 → (1/3, 1/3, 1/3). No transitions leave P, so that row has no data.
  • (c) AFFAFP + AFTATP + AFPAPP = (1/3)(0) + (2/3)(1/3) + 0 = 2/9 ≈ 0.22
